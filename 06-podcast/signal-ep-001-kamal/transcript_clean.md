## The opener

**SPEAKER:** Hi everybody, this is The Signal — a podcast with recruiters and recruitment founders, having good conversations to understand what they do, how they work, and how it correlates to the wider audience. Today we have Kamal. I'm here with my co-founder Malik. We're going to get straight into it.

Kamal — 60 seconds. What do you do, who do you serve?

**KAMAL:** My name's Kamal — great introduction, thank you. I'm a recruiter with experience in both agency and RPO. I've been doing it about eleven years across different markets — IT, finance. Now I'm on the internal side, which I'd say is a lot easier than agency. That's me in 60 seconds.

**SPEAKER:** Expand a bit — what are you focused on right now, and what got you to that state?

**KAMAL:** I work for an RPO, but I recruit on behalf of one of their major tech clients. I'm fully remote, which is a blessing. My role is to focus on software engineers at a certain level across Europe — UK, Dublin, Switzerland, Romania, Poland, anywhere in the EU. Those are the main hubs.

## Origin: biochem, a surprise, and a cousin in recruitment

**SPEAKER:** Recruiting at that level is mental — that's a case in itself. We've done research on you, but no one else understands the journey. Coming from Harlesden into that corporate world — that's a wide gap. What got you here?

**KAMAL:** I think most recruiters will tell you they didn't plan to go into recruitment. They just landed in it. I was studying biochemistry at university. I finished my degree, graduated — and shortly after, I had a surprise. My son was arriving, which was a big surprise for me and my missus. That led to the mindset of: *I just want to make money quickly.*

I had a cousin in recruitment. Same temperament, same vibe. He said, "Give it a go." So I started applying. Got my first role — bit of a mad company, especially as your first real working opportunity out of uni. Weird scenarios. Left on my own accord, but I met someone there who introduced me to my next company — Frank Recruitment Group, where I spent the bulk of my career.

**SPEAKER:** I've got two little girls — I know that mindset shift. *I need to make money now.* It's massive. So recruitment landed in your lap. But what made you think this was a career you could make money in based on the skill set you had?

**KAMAL:** I was thinking about this exact scenario — my skill set. Naturally, I'm not a salesperson. Going into recruitment, you might think it's admin-based, very conversational. "Do you want a job?" But it's a full-on sales role, and most agencies looking to make money need salespeople.

So from day one, I was out of my comfort zone. I know how to speak to people — I'm not an awkward communicator — but translating that into sales is something completely different. My first impression was: *I need to change fast. I need to get comfortable having certain conversations with stakeholders.*

In terms of realising it was a career I'd stick with — recruitment's a funny one. You do well in one month and that keeps you there for the next. You've got commission coming in three months' time, so you can't leave — you're waiting for that three or six grand. And within that period you've got to deliver, because you've got to keep your job. It's a constant chase of the next paycheck.

After a while it just became my reality. I got more comfortable, better at the job, more successful. You're hitting promotions, or you're close to one, so you don't want to leave before you get it. Naturally, six years pass, and you're a recruiter.

## The solo agency that didn't work

**SPEAKER:** So where was the first pivot? You started your own thing for six months — where did that come from, and why the move from there to something else?

**KAMAL:** Brutally honest — I'd been doing agency a long time. The company I was at was small compared to what I was used to. I decided it was time to move. I had good options interviewing elsewhere, but with the money I'd seen you could make from that market, I thought I'd give it a go on my own. I'd established good clients I knew would come with me. Once you've got a client base, you're in a good position — or that's what I thought.

The reality was completely different. The cost of running your own agency was something I didn't anticipate. I'd made good money over the years but wasn't someone who saved for a rainy day. I thought six or seven grand — that's fine. A LinkedIn Recruiter licence alone was about £700 a month. You can't recruit without it. But your money dwindles, and you haven't done that first deal to give you the injection.

The idea for me was: if you're not making good money in recruitment, it's not worth doing the job. The benefit of recruitment is that you *can* make good money. It's not a job that requires a great educational background — gift of the gab and you can make money. So at five, six months in, I'm not making the money. My son's getting older, more demands. I needed to get back into the corporate world where it's consistent.

**SPEAKER:** When you said existing clients would come with you — was the issue that you didn't have a consistent pipeline of new clients? Or that you couldn't place candidates with the clients you had?

**KAMAL:** A mixture. Forensic accounting is niche. Everyone knows everyone, everyone's aware of the companies. The client base I had — it wasn't the biggest. It was enough, but trying to place candidates into that specific base was a tall order. So I needed BD to generate new clients to give my candidates more options. But the competition, and I'm a one-man band — no marketing, no branding, on a budget. Every day was: you're trying to do BD, you're not covering ground. Sometimes BD isn't a one-month job — it's a six-month job to even get your foot in the door.

**SPEAKER:** We have this with our clients — they expect things instantaneously, but it's a cycle. If you've got a client bringing you 60–70 grand a year in fees, there's a cost to build that relationship — time, money, or both. With the 360 recruiter idea — in the past, when people slammed the phones, you had a massive function. Nowadays everything's automated, the competition is massive — you need systems to expand. You can't be caught in feast-and-famine.

**KAMAL:** A hundred percent. Even slamming the phones — that culture's gone. COVID took a lot of that away. The world's changed. A lot of recruitment owners — guys in their 40s, 50s, from that old-school way — aren't adjusting to things becoming digital and automated. That's where a lot of them aren't seeing success.

**SPEAKER:** The reason a lot of agencies don't grow — and aren't sellable — is because the founders are the ones doing BD. They're reliant on bringing in the clients themselves. That model can't run in this day and age. You need people who are hungry, fresh, and understand the climate.

## Niche markets, and the school they put you through

**SPEAKER:** Tell me about the niche thing. What does that niche even cover, and how do you become good at finding candidates that match the standard of a top-tier client?

**KAMAL:** I've always worked in markets where the product isn't necessarily the biggest or most popular. The pool of candidates is smaller, which means the BD pool is smaller too — fewer people to reach out to with a role. I worked in ERP — financial accounting systems. Specifically things like SAP and Sage. Compared to Microsoft products, NetSuite — it was harder to navigate because everyone knows each other, everyone's aware of the company. Selling becomes more challenging because they already know.

But working in those spaces trains you from a sales perspective. It means you just have to be better at sales. It's harder to place these people, harder to find them. So when you do get hold of someone, your sell has to be almost perfect. That's how you get better at the role.

I always say — Frank Recruitment Group, as much as it was a hard environment to work in at times, in terms of training and sales experience, it was A1. Turns you into great recruiters if you're prepared to put the graft in. So working in those spaces becomes the norm. Sometimes you wait six months until they've had a bad day at their current job and they're prepared to speak to you. Some people, twelve months. Every day in recruitment is different.

Now, recruiting at that level — you're looking for a certain standard of candidate. It's tough. But your experience of working in these spaces is: I can find them. I know where to look, I know the reach-out I need to do. And then at the end, it's a conversation. That's the difference now versus agency. What I'm doing now is more conversational. I don't feel like a salesperson. I still have to be authentic, give the best overview, the reality — but it's not that hard sales I was doing before.

## What broke you, what built you

**SPEAKER:** You've had an extensive journey. What's been the most challenging part? Most people I speak to say at six months or a year in, they wanted to quit. To persist to where you are now means you were resilient — strong mindset.

**KAMAL:** Most people would have felt that way at some point. I had a hard first two years. My first year at Frank was actually quite successful for a newbie — but it's the adjustment. It's working 7:45 to 9pm. That's an expectation. Not "you'd get fired if you don't" — but they really drilled the idea that you'll get out what you put in. It gets you in that mindset. At the time I had a young kid at home, I'm feeling like I'm missing a lot — but at the same time, *I'm doing this for my son.* So you get into the 12-hour days.

There were times where it was: you know what, I just want to find a graduate job and get out. A nine-to-five. But you see the financial successes. If you're motivated by money, that will keep you going. I've seen young boys, 21, 22, who've landed on the right desk — they're doing eight, ten grand a month. To someone that age, that's life-changing. They feel validated by the money. But some people can't get past the barrier of doing the work.

**SPEAKER:** And you see it more now in agencies because the younger generation is different.

**KAMAL:** I'll let every generation be who they are — they would've said stuff about us too. But I took a stint away from Frank, then went back. Seeing the change in mentality — it was nothing like before. People getting up 5:30 on the dot, gone. You're like, that's not how you make money in this role. Some people will take six months to do their first deal. The first six months you really want it, and it's hard, and you'll see a lot of people bow out. But when you get to that first deal — they talk about it in recruitment, the *penny drops* — that's it.

**SPEAKER:** It's reinforcing behaviour. That's our whole ethos. If you can get something right once, you can replicate it. Once you can replicate it, you've got the skill.

## 360 vs candidate-only

**SPEAKER:** Right now you're focused on the candidate side at the RPO. That's a job in itself — probably the hardest job in recruitment, finding the best candidate. But switching to BD is completely different. How did you find being a 360 recruiter compared to what you do now?

**KAMAL:** Breath of fresh air, honestly. I always liked BD, but it's one of the hardest things to do from the agency side. You go through so many no's before you get a yes. And sometimes when you get a yes, it's not even a yes — it's half a yes, from the wrong person, but you take it because you've been at it for time. You speak to someone in finance — sounds really good — they go to the finance manager, no sign-off, can't work with us. As much as you enjoy it, that's where the variety of the role is. If you only focused on candidates in agency, I think you'd get bored quickly. BD is where the changing conversations come from.

But it's tough. The idea of 60, 70 dials in an hour — most of the time you're not getting through to the right person. Up, down, up, down, like Wall Street. Every company I worked at had a dedicated BD time block. I think it's positive in theory, but you get people who fall into the pattern of pulling up the same list they pulled up last week, because they know no one's going to pick up — but they'll have done the 60, 70 dials.

**SPEAKER:** Then what's the point? Why are they even in the role?

**KAMAL:** You'd question a lot of people's motivations. Some people just like a steady base. If they can put the figures up, they're fine for a while. Others have genuine motivation. You can fluctuate — you're human. But going from that to where I'm just on the candidate side now, it makes the day less stressful. You know what you're saying to candidates, you know the roles. There isn't that variety, so it depends what you're looking for.

## Top billers, integrity, and cowboy recruiting

**SPEAKER:** There's a clear difference between someone who's a good biller and someone who isn't, and between someone who's good at finding candidates and someone who isn't. What's the split between someone good at BD versus someone good at recruiting? What are you the best at?

**KAMAL:** Being transparent — I'm someone who's driven for success. I like being in the conversation. But I don't have that cutthroat nature I see top billers having. It's not part of my nature to step on someone to get what I'm looking for, or to squeeze a candidate into a job where I know it's not going to work out — but it'll get me through the rebate period so the client doesn't get money back.

You see that type of stuff. I'm not judgmental — but it's not best practice. That client's not coming back when they realise. Top billers have a different mentality. Their brain is different. They have to make something happen every day. I admire it. I'm just not wired that way. The clear difference is the work rate, the hunger and desire. But every top biller I've encountered has that streak where you're questioning their integrity. One of my closest friends in my recruitment career — like a brother — was one of the best recruiters I've met. But a lot of people didn't like him.

**SPEAKER:** The reason BD has changed and competition keeps getting higher is that the trust element is gone. People don't want to work with agencies for that exact reason. You've got people with the gift of the gab who can get through the client's door, and once they're in, they don't care about the candidate side.

**KAMAL:** Cowboy recruiting. I've seen recruiters get the praise — they've billed X amount, smashing it. Imagine you're a contractor, I'm your recruiter, I've placed you on a client site, and I'm making more money than you are as a day rate.

**SPEAKER:** Have you seen that?

**KAMAL:** Listen, I've seen madness. 200/800 splits. 300/700. The client only clocks on if the contractor and the manager go for a drink and discuss pay. The manager says "the agency's paying you 200?" — then the manager realises they're paying the agency a grand.

**SPEAKER:** Before this, I was an analyst — getting contract rates — and there were odd moments where the pay grade I was on didn't match what I was being held accountable for. Something must have been going on.

**KAMAL:** And there are recruiters I respect that take a fair margin. But some of them — as long as they're getting their money. That's why recruitment has the stigma it has, especially in the UK. In Europe and America, being a recruiter or headhunter is a respected position. They're getting big base salaries and making good money because they're respected. In the UK, companies hate recruiters. That's why BD is so hard. They get a million calls a day, same chat, and they've been stung by an agency a month ago. So now they don't want to work with us. You've got genuine recruiters out there saying "I've got the perfect candidate" — and you really might — but because of their experience with the last guy…

**SPEAKER:** "The perfect candidate" has been used too many times.

**KAMAL:** Literally. Top billers have that streak — they know they're never going to work with that client again, so they'll charge them 15 instead of 10. Could be a small family company on a limited budget. We're going to milk them if we can.

## Where it's going: COVID, AI, and the trust gap

**SPEAKER:** Where do you feel it's going? You've seen pre-COVID, post-COVID — what's your opinion on the direction?

**KAMAL:** COVID was the biggest shift. Everything shut down, and then maybe a year or two after, the market boomed — everyone needs something, we're back, I need ten rather than five. And then it dropped right back down. Since then I know a lot of agencies have shut down. Smaller firms have struggled, big companies have gone off the map.

Automation is a massive piece. If you get it right, it can fly. The idea of a company relying on software that's less fee-focused — if you get it right, it cuts out the whole agency interaction, which a lot of companies don't enjoy doing anyway. It's needed, because right now no one's bridged the gap between the human element and identifying not just a candidate for the skill set but doing the extra stuff on top. If you can bridge that, the agency becomes irrelevant.

There will be recruiters that hate the idea — but it can't be helped. The way AI is moving, I'm looking at videos on TikTok and Instagram and questioning what I'm seeing. If you can use those tools to automate CV screening — cutting out the part that's long for the hiring manager — you've got 150 applicants, 90% can't get sponsorship, you've got to go through all of that. If you can get something that says "these are the five you should call, here's why" — that's it.

**SPEAKER:** That's exactly what we did. We're at capacity because the space is so needed and everybody wants it. We're essentially selling shovels in a gold rush. With code, anything can be done. But the human element you mentioned is important — recruitment is a human-centric business. You need humans involved.

Other people who've tried to solve the candidate-driven side and the BD side have gone full AI or full systems. It's not going to work, because people reciprocate with people. If you're reaching out with an automated message that's got little dashes in it from ChatGPT, people see it from a mile away. Whereas if you've got a team responding to the system's call with the information, it's a completely different conversation — you can be context-aware and have the conversation with the right person at the right time on the right day.

**KAMAL:** It's a tough one to crack, but it sounds like you're on the right track. The fine-tuning is going to happen through trial and error.

**SPEAKER:** Do you feel agencies that have failed did so because of their reactivity rather than their proactiveness?

**KAMAL:** The ones that went away — it's their lack of ability to adapt. What I would have seen and heard is: "Things are really bad. Do more of the same. You just have to do more of the same." And that's genuine. But if the solution is *do ten times more of what we're doing* — we're doing ten times more of the wrong thing.

**SPEAKER:** People who started their agencies forty, fifty years ago — their neural connections are wired to one route. Now everything's changing with AI, the trust is going down. It has to be reset, otherwise you're not going to grow. From the conversations we have with people we work with, as soon as we explain how we operate and why it's worked, you see the penny drop. *Shit. That's why it's not been working.*

## The next big change

**SPEAKER:** When do you feel the next big change is coming?

**KAMAL:** I'd love to say I know but I don't, and I think it's because I'm not in the agency space. AI tools wouldn't only be relevant to agency, but the benefit of an RPO team is different — you've got a set fee or arrangement. How hard the RPO team works isn't going to change. They always have to be on the go. Whereas agency is more reactionary in my opinion. People might work really hard, or they might not — they've got the gift of the gab and only really start working when they get a new client, because the client's been waiting two months.

The changing factor is the people — hopefully you lot — who figure out the gap.

**SPEAKER:** I believe in us. A lot of people are trying it, but we've seen where they don't have it figured out — overextending, overspending. With AI, the stigma is that it'll replace humans. But if you can include a human layer while enhancing everything around the business — get that blend right, maintain the human interaction for the conversations that need it, the closing of a candidate — and have most of the process automated, it's an easy life. And profitable.

**KAMAL:** Hundred percent.

**SPEAKER:** AI is centred around data. If you can get AI to do the systems work and humans to interact, you've solved the problem. But it's also about the capacity and skill of the individual doing the calls. Without a good recruiter on the back end of the system, it's always going to fail.

**KAMAL:** Yeah, exactly.

**SPEAKER:** When we first started, we tried to use AI for everything. Didn't work. We're now implementing things that introduce human layers — and the results we've seen, penny drop.

## Context-rich BD: the way it actually works

**SPEAKER:** Currently in your role, was that always the niche, or did you fall into it?

**KAMAL:** No — I've never even worked with the programming languages I'm working with now. But because of my background in so many other spaces, you become versatile. To be fair, it happened relatively early in my career. I don't necessarily need to understand your tech — I need to understand what you're looking for. As long as I've got the buzzwords and what's important and what's not, I'll qualify the candidate. To be a good recruiter, it's better to have a deeper understanding — but you can have different levels of conversation to qualify.

**SPEAKER:** Where do BD reps who uncover that context currently get it from? Because if you show up knowing — "you guys have just started this project, you've got funding from this place, you're building out your program" — that initiates a better conversation. Where do they get that context?

**KAMAL:** It's the training. The quality of the qualification is going to give better results, I promise you. One of the best placements I ever did came from visiting the client face-to-face. As soon as I got back to the office, I knew what he was looking for to a depth I didn't have for roles where I'd had a five- or ten-minute qualification call.

When you go into that deep understanding — not just skill set but interpersonal, soft skills — what you can deliver is way better than the vague overview. I can speak to a client, do a five-minute qualification, send a hundred CVs — no one's the right fit. But if I've spent 30, 45 minutes with that client, I can send five and three out of five will be suitable. The more you understand, the better. That was always drilled into us — qualification calls 30 to 45 minutes minimum.

**SPEAKER:** How do you even get into the qualification call in the first place? The example you gave — you sat down with the client. What initiated the conversation? If you'd gone in with context on what they wanted, would it have put you in better stead?

**KAMAL:** With that specific role they had an advert out. So I knew they were hiring, knew what they were looking for, had three people I'd already spoken to that would fit. The better level comes from communication. If you've got a client you're trying to crack, I might have spoken to someone on that hiring manager's team — casual conversation. Where are you at, what are you doing, what's coming up? They give me current projects, upcoming projects, what they'd be looking for as a company. By the time you go to the hiring manager, you're educated already. That's where the agency side of it comes in.

**SPEAKER:** When I speak to recruiters that don't use any systems, they say what you've just said — speak to a candidate, they tell you the projects. But this information is *out there*.

**KAMAL:** It is.

**SPEAKER:** If you're aware — the job ad is a trigger to have the conversation, but what about everything that comes before? If you've captured that context, the conversation after is way easier. You can say to them, *we know this project is coming up, we just saw the post, we've been monitoring you, I've got these candidates.*

**KAMAL:** That's how contract recruiters operate. There's a difference in temperament between perm and contract recruiters. Perm are more hand-holding — *how does your partner feel about you changing job, how is this going to affect your dog.* That's the truth. I've had candidates who won't relocate because they can't bring their dog. Contract recruiters' whole business is structured off getting valuable project information. They have to stay on top of what projects are happening so they can put five contractors in front of you before you even know you need them. Their thing is 90% BD — the easiest part is placing the candidate once they get the job. In perm, the hardest thing is finding the candidate — but you always have the job.

**SPEAKER:** With what we're doing, our mindset has to shift into a recruitment pattern — but we look at it from a systems perspective first. Everything you've described is, in my mind, a system of steps. If you can gain context of those steps and pull information to fill those boxes, the conversation becomes easier regardless of contract or perm. The contract example you gave is great. If the project information is out there and you can pull it through a system, the conversation when you see a job post for someone you haven't worked with before is so much easier. They look at you and think, *how did this guy know that?*

**KAMAL:** He's a good recruiter.

**SPEAKER:** And there's trust there.

## Some people can read off a sheet. Some can't.

**KAMAL:** Even with AI — the information has to be somewhere first. The other day I'm waiting for the gym I've signed up to to go to tier two. I went on ChatGPT — *when is this happening, is it actually happening?* It didn't give me a specific day, but it found all the information around it.

**SPEAKER:** That's exactly what we do. We track everything about our client. That gives us enough context to apply to our reps. Whenever we're having a conversation, it's happening for a reason. It's not off the back of a reactive post — if we don't have enough information, the conversation doesn't hold weight.

**KAMAL:** Especially when you're reaching out to someone not already in your network. If you've got a sharp person who picks up the phone, you've got two minutes to show you're good at your job.

**SPEAKER:** And when you're able to apply that context, it works. But we've run through people, we've seen — some people don't have the ability to read off a sheet and turn it into a conversation. We listen to the calls and it's: that's not going to work. We have to get someone else in.

**KAMAL:** That's the difference between a good recruiter and a bad one. Some people have the gift of the gab. One thing I've always been good at — if I don't know what I'm talking about, it'll still sound like I do. I'm not unique. Plenty of people in recruitment can blag it. But to provide a premium level of service, you want someone who can read it, understand it, and use it as a prompt while they're on the call.

**SPEAKER:** If you've got the gift of the gab, you've got the capacity to understand. So when you're speaking, it's coming from somewhere. You can't just be making it up. When you've got the additional context and you actually understand it — it solidifies you. You're no longer a rambler.

**KAMAL:** Some of the recruiters I've met in my career are highly intelligent individuals. Visibly even how they speak — not the smoothest talkers, but the level of conversation they could have because they understood the tech was way better than 90% of the sales floor. They'd be successful, but the issue with those types is they know they're smart and go off to do something that requires more brain power. You see them in six months at a stint and they're doing two deals in the second month, made 30 grand — because they can have a conversation at a higher level. It's not about volume. It's about quality. Ten high-level conversations and three of them convert into business.

## What recruiters get wrong

**SPEAKER:** What belief do recruiters have that they've got wrong?

**KAMAL:** Tough one. I'd say it's not all recruiters — it's management. In agency you get promoted off numbers. Start as a recruiter, banging year — senior. Another banging year — principal. Now you're a team leader, responsible for management. Promoting off numbers isn't a good system. A lot of agencies look at someone and say, "they've done really well as a recruiter, so they'll be a good manager." Not always the case.

So the belief that someone is going to be a good leader because they were a good recruiter — that's a big myth in agencies. That's where a lot of agencies go wrong. I would have had managers in the past — A1 recruiters in terms of billing — but when it came to management, they didn't know how to speak to people, manage people, or get the best out of people. If you're going into leadership, you need clear qualities of a leader.

## The worst day

**SPEAKER:** Best day versus worst day in recruitment?

**KAMAL:** If I think about the worst day — there's probably five days I could go on the back of. I'll keep this one surface level. Actually, no — I'll give you the full context.

I had a client looking for a finance manager up north. I'd been sourcing for a week. I had about five candidates. I sent them to the client, set up a feedback call. We've got on the phone, gone through them, and the client has gone for the candidate asking for the least amount of money. Saving costs, no problem.

Being the good recruiter that I am, I said, "I understand why you're picking that, but I'd also recommend interviewing these three, because I know that candidate lacks X, Y, and Z." No, it's fine — I'll interview this one, looks fine on paper. Interviewed, hired. Job done.

A couple of weeks later, immediately available, they started. They've been let go. Apparently they messed up something big at month-end. Now obviously that falls within — actually, I'm lying. It was just outside of the rebate period. So technically I had no obligation. Realistically, I could have turned around and said sorry mate — and I told you to interview the others. I did the right thing. But out of goodwill, my manager said *find them a replacement.* Which means I'm now spending time finding someone I'm not going to make any money off of. Big investment of my time for no benefit.

I had a team leader who had a meeting with me about my focus for the day. I said, "I need to go and replace this job." No. The team leader turned around and said, "That's not the best use of your time. It's not going to make you any money." Cool. You're my manager, no problem.

Two weeks later, the big manager has had an email from the client saying *what's going on, we haven't had any replacement CVs.* The team leader and the manager hadn't spoken about what happened. So the big manager comes to me — *why haven't you been sending any CVs?* I said, "I went to the team leader and they told me not to."

The big manager — five-foot-something, small guy — got up and started slamming his head off the desk. Full force. He's got up, slamming his head off the desk. The last one was so hard he must have semi-passed out. He's falling back. He's on the floor on his back. I'm sitting at my desk. He's got up, pointing at me, forehead red, semi-dazed: "*This is what you make me want to do.*"

That guy had taught me some of the best things I've learned as a recruiter, but he was also the biggest problem I had in my career. Where I'm from — I've never claimed to be a certain type of person, but your life experiences teach you to accept certain things. When you're in a corporate environment and you didn't necessarily come from this world, you're tested a lot. You spend a lot of time biting your tongue thinking *I don't want to lose my job.* You're maintaining professionalism, but at the same time — that interaction bypasses the trigger. You can't even respond in your normal way.

I ended up leaving. The reason it's my worst is being in that environment for so long — you know the hours you've put in, the sacrifice, the times you've had a bad six months, missing stuff with your kid for this role — and then you're being treated like that. I don't feel valued. I've never been one to make noise. I could have gone to HR fifty times about things that happened in that place. I've always just been *let me do my job.* But that day made recruitment not worth what I'd been through.

**SPEAKER:** What's the key thing you took away from it?

**KAMAL:** Resilience — I came back the next day. But the bigger lesson: *don't compromise yourself.* For people who came into the business after me — the friends I've got to this day — what I'd say to them is, *don't let no one take you for a clown in this place.* For me, that was one of my big struggles. I know who I am. I've never been an idiot for anybody. But there were moments where I'd question — *did you stay true to yourself?* Reflecting now, there'd never be an instance where you could try to mug me off in a meeting. I'd turn around and ask, "What did you mean by that? Could you clarify?" That's how you're supposed to be. People take liberties because they've gotten away with it before.

My biggest learning was always: stay true to yourself, regardless of who is who. Recruiters have this thing where because they're the top biller in the building, they think they're a bigger person in life than you. Those are the top billers I'm referring to with that streak. That experience showed me — never let anyone violate you again in a work setting. Keep it professional. Respectful.

## AI in five years

**SPEAKER:** Where do you see AI being most powerful in recruitment within the next five years?

**KAMAL:** Sourcing is going to be massive. I think AI will get so strong that a hiring manager could sign up to a service, put in their job ad, and AI would give them ten people to reach out to. To what extent of their availability would vary. But if you fine-tune it, AI could send the outreach message and schedule the conversation between the candidate and the hiring manager if there's interest. The sourcing element will completely take over.

**SPEAKER:** Why do you think hiring managers struggle and end up taking on agencies?

**KAMAL:** A lot of hiring managers can't be bothered. When you get a hiring manager that's happy to work with you, it's because they don't want the workload. If they had the capacity to do it themselves, they would. But sitting down and going through the people that have applied online — most applicants for a job aren't suitable. I could write an advert as a recruitment agent and 90% of what comes through won't be relevant. A hiring manager doing that, when it's not actually their job — they've got to manage the technical team — that's long.

The other reason is niche skill sets. People with a small part of their skill set that is super relevant to a project. In those two scenarios, they'll go to agencies. Or volume-without-fit issues — cultural, interpersonal. That's where the human element ensures it's not a thing, because AI isn't going to vet someone's personality.

**SPEAKER:** Doing the candidate search now — do you get bombarded with AI-generated CVs?

**KAMAL:** Honestly, no. The people applying for the roles I'm in are quite perfect in their approach. The CV needs to look good. They believe everything affects their application. So I haven't really encountered profiles that look generated.

**SPEAKER:** Or maybe they're so good that you can't tell. Back in the day, I created a bot that would tailor my experience and CV and apply to every job out there.

**KAMAL:** That's the thing. Sometimes it can't be spotted. At that tier, the standard is another level.

## Selling candidates through process

**SPEAKER:** When you've found the right person, what's your approach to seeing them through the process?

**KAMAL:** Their processes are quite tight, so there's only so far I can go. Preparation — making sure they're clear on what to expect technically, plus tips on how to interview, which is so important. There are professionals out there who have the skills for a job but how they interview is why they don't get it. Especially in tech, people think "if you're a developer, you can do the job, you'll get it." But companies are looking for someone they can have a chat with — articulate, fits with the team. A lot of people have an idea of devs as just coders, but a lot of the people I speak to are outgoing, well-spoken, articulate.

In my role now, it's about transparency. Keeping them updated. Letting their technical skills take them from there. As long as I cover the interpersonal side, if they're good enough technically, we'll get there. There's nothing I can do about candidates not being up to standard on the tech. From an agency perspective, there's way more room — because you're selling. If a hiring manager comes back saying "I've interviewed them but…" — your job is to make their reservations seem smaller. That's where you make your money in agency, turning those into yeses.

## Inside the RPO model

**SPEAKER:** How do RPOs go about BD?

**KAMAL:** Honestly — I don't fully know. I've interviewed for RPOs in the past and didn't get them because I didn't have RPO experience. I was fortunate with my position now. They probably have ways of proving worth — *give us your ten hardest roles, we'll fill them for free.* If you land the contract and they're paying you 500 grand a year, ten placements is light work. And then it's about delivering — if you're an RPO, you've got the manpower. *Listen, we're trying to land this big account at GSK — I need fifty of us taken off whatever you're doing to resource, we've got to fill these ten roles to get the account done.* That's how they operate.
